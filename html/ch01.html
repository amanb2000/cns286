Probability distributions quantify the likelihood of events. Modern language models are chiefly concerned with approximating the probability distribution of sequences of well-formed text. Once a high-quality approximation of the probability distribution of text sequences is learned, text can be generated by sampling the distribution. A basic understanding of probability distributions (Section 1.1) is key to working with large language models. We will then provide an overview of basic methods (ML, MAP) for estimating probability distributions in Section 1.2.

1.1: Probability Spaces

Definition: Probability space – a probability space is a mathematical framework to model random experiments that consists of three elements:

  1. Sample space Ω\Omega: The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.
  2. Sigma-algebra (ℱ\mathcal F): A collection of subsets of Ω\Omega including Ω\Omega itself and the empty set ⌀\varnothing that satisfies the axioms of σ\sigma-algebras (e.g., closure under complement and countable unions), representing the events or “outcomes” we can assign probabilites to.
  3. Probability measure (PP): A function P:ℱ→[0,1]P: \mathcal F \to [0, 1] that assigns probabilities to events in ℱ\mathcal F.

The probability measure PP must satisfy the Kolmogorov axioms:

  1. Non-negativity: For any event A∈ℱA \in \mathcal F, P(A)≥0P(A) \geq 0
  2. Normalization: P(Ω)=1P(\Omega) = 1.
  3. Countable Additivity: For any countable sequence of pairwise disjoint events {Ai}i=1∞⊂ℱ\{A_i\}_{i=1}^\infty \subset \mathcal F (i.e. Ai∩Aj=⌀A_i \cap A_j = \varnothing for i≠ji\neq j).

These axioms set the stage for deriving important properties of probability distributions. E.g., for any two events A,B∈ℱA, B \in \mathcal F, P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)

Random Variables

Definition: Random variable – a random variable is a measurable function that maps outcomes from a random experiment to real numbers: X:Ω→ℝX: \Omega \to \mathbb R Random variables quantify random phenomena and are central to probability and statistics.

  • Discrete random variables: Take on countable values (e.g., integers).
  • Continuous random variables: Take on values from an interval or collection of intervals in ℝ\mathbb R.

Notation: Statistics and probability theory might has irritating notation, but we are stuck with it.

  • Uppercase letters (e.g., XX) denote random variables.
  • Lowercase letters (e.g., xx) denote specific values or “realizations” of the random variable XX. We might say xx is sampled from XX or a function of XX as x∼Xx\sim X or x∼g(X)x \sim g(X). You may also see x∼fX(x)x\sim f_X(x) or x∼pX(x)x\sim p_X(x) when attention is being drawn to the probability distribution of random variable XX.

Definition: Support of random variable SXS_X – the support or range of a random variable XX is SX={x∈ℝ∣∃ω∈Ω such that X(ω)=x}S_X = \{x \in \mathbb R \mid \exists \omega \in \Omega \text{ such that } X(\omega) = x\}

1.2 Probability Distributions

A probability distribution describes how probabilities are assigned over the potential values of a random variable.

Discrete Random Variables and Probability Mass Functions (PMFs)

For a discrete random variable XX with support SXS_X, the probability mass function (PMF) pX(x)p_X(x) gives the probability Pr{X=x}\Pr\{X = x\}. PMFs obey the following properties:

  1. Non-negativity: pX(x)≥0p_X(x) \geq 0 ∀\forall x∈SXx \in S_X.
  2. Normalization: ∑x∈SXpX(x)=1\sum_{x\in S_X} p_X(x) = 1
  3. Additivity: For any countable collection of disjoint values {xi}\{x_i\}, Pr(X∈{xi})=∑xipX(xi)\Pr \big( X \in \{x_i\} \big) = \sum_{x_i} p_X(x_i)

Continuous Random Variables and Probability Density Functions

For a continuous random variable XX, the probability density function (PDF) fX(x)f_X(x) is a function such that the probability such that XX falls within interval [a,b][a, b] is given by Pr(a≤X≤b)=∫abfX(x)dx\Pr\big(a \leq X \leq b\big) = \int_a^b f_X(x) dx

Properties of PDFs:

  1. Non-negativity: fX(x)≥0f_X(x) \geq 0 ∀x∈ℝ\forall x \in \mathbb R
  2. Normalization: ∫−∞∞fX(x)dx=1\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) dx = 1
  3. Probability over intervals: Probabilities are calculated over intervals, not at individual points as applying Pr(a≤X≤b)=∫abfX(x)dx\Pr\big(a \leq X \leq b\big) = \int_a^b f_X(x) dx to an interval of zero length gives zero probability.

The name for the value of the PDF fX(x)f_X(x) at a particular value of xx is called “likelihood” rather than “probability”. If you confuse these, it will anger the statisticians.

Laws of Large Numbers

Recall the definitions of expected value for continuous random variable XX is μ=𝔼[X]=∫−∞∞x⋅fX(x)\mu = \mathbb E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f_X(x). For XX, we have μ=𝔼[X]=∑x∈SXx⋅pX(x)\mu = \mathbb E[X] = \sum_{x\in S_X} x\cdot p_X(x).

Weak law of large numbers (Khinchin’s law): Let {Xi}i=1n\{X_i\}_{i=1}^n be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with finite expected value μ=𝔼[Xi]\mu = \mathbb E[X_i]. Then, for any ϵ>0\epsilon > 0, limn→∞Pr(|1n∑i=1nXi−μ|≥ϵ)=0 \lim_{n\to \infty} \Pr\begin{pmatrix} \big| \frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n X_i - \mu \big| \geq \epsilon \end{pmatrix} = 0

Strong law of large numbers: If {xi}i=1n\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n are sampled from i.i.d. random variables with mean μ=𝔼[Xi]\mu = \mathbb E[X_i], then the probability of the sample mean = μ\mu is 11 as n→∞n \to \infty. Pr(limn→∞1n∑i=1nXi=μ)=1 \Pr\begin{pmatrix} \lim_{n\to \infty} \frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n X_i = \mu \end{pmatrix} = 1

Conditional Distributions

Joint distributions for two random variables are denoted as fXY(x,y)f_{XY}(x, y) for continuous random variables and pXY(x,y)p_{XY}(x, y). For brevity, we will refer to each without the subscripts as f(x,y)f(x, y) and p(x,y)p(x, y). Conditional distributions are denoted f(y|x)f(y | x) or p(y|x)p(y | x) and are interpreted as the probability distribution over yy given a known value of xx.

f(y|x)=f(x,y)/f(x) f(y | x) = f(x, y) / f(x)

p(y|x)=p(x,y)/p(x) p(y | x) = p(x, y) / p(x)

Independence: Random variables X,YX, Y are independent iff p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)p(x, y) = p(x) p(y). This also implies that p(y|x)=p(y)p(y | x) = p(y).

Chain Rule of Probability

Let us break free from the assumption of independence in a sequence of random variables {Xi}i=1n\{X_i\}_{i=1}^n. If we know that the process underlying {Xi}\{X_i\} is causal – i.e., XiX_i is conditional only on X1,…,Xi−1X_1, \dots, X_{i-1}, then we can use this handy trick to factor the joint probability of the sequence p(x1,…,xn)p(x_1, \dots, x_n). as

p(x1,…,xn)=∏i=1np(xi|x<i) p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i | x_{< i})

where x<ix_{< i} denotes x1,…,xi−1x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}. The chain rule of probability is especially handy for modelling language as a random process.

Derivation: By repeatedly applying the chain rule of probability and invoking our causal assumption (XiX_i is only conditional on X<iX_{< i}), we get

p(x1)=p(x1) p(x_1) = p(x_1)

p(x1,x2)=p(x2|x1)p(x1) p(x_1, x_2) = p(x_2 | x_1) p(x_1)

p(x1,x2,x3)=p(x3|x1,x2)p(x1,x2) p(x_1, x_2, x_3) = p(x_3 | x_1, x_2) p(x_1, x_2)

=p(x3|x1,x2)p(x2|x1)p(x1)⏟≡p(x1,x2) = p(x_3 | x_1, x_2) \underbrace{p(x_2 | x_1) p(x_1)}_{\equiv p(x_1, x_2)}

And so on, until we derive the general form.

Note: While I have used discrete/PMF notation p(x1,…,xn)p(x_1, \dots, x_n), the chain rule also applies to causal random processes with continuous variables f(x1,…,xn)f(x_1, \dots, x_n).

1.2: Estimating Probability Distributions

Abstract probability distributions like p(x)p(x), p(y|x)p(y | x), and p(xi|x1,…,xi−1)p(x_i | x_1,\dots, x_{i-1}) are great to think about, but we often are not given the exact form of p(x)p(x) initially. More commonly, we have a bunch of samples from the distribution in question, and we need to estimate p(x)p(x) from samples xi∼p(x)x_i \sim p(x) or xi∼f(x)x_i \sim f(x).

Parameterized probability distributions: The default variable to represent the parameters of a probability distribution is θ\theta, and the parameterized distribution as p(x;θ)p(x; \theta) or f(x;θ)f(x; \theta). You may also see the equivalent notation pθ(x)p_{\theta}(x) or fθ(x)f_{\theta}(x) commonly used.

Goal of parameter estimation: Once we have figured out which family of distributions we are interested in, we want to estimate the best parameters θ̂\hat \theta that explain our measurements, where θ̂\hat \theta is an estimator for the true parameter θ\theta.

Types of estimators: Once you derive an estimator, these concepts are useful for understanding how good you can expect your estimator to be. Let θ̂n\hat \theta_n represent the estimator of θ\theta given nn samples from the “ground truth” distribution (e.g., experimental measurements).

  1. Estimation error: θ̂n−θ\hat \theta_n - \theta
  2. Bias of estimator: 𝔼[θ̂n]−θ\mathbb E[\hat \theta_n] - \theta
    • Unbiased estimator: 𝔼[θ̂n]=θ\mathbb E[\hat \theta_n] = \theta for all θ\theta.
    • Asymptotically unbiased estimator: limn→∞𝔼[θ̂n]=θ\lim_{n\to \infty} \mathbb E[\hat \theta_n] = \theta for all θ\theta.
  3. Consistency: An estimator is consistent if limn→∞θ̂n=θ\lim_{n\to \infty} \hat \theta_n = \theta.

Simplest parameter estimation example: Let’s say we have a bunch of i.i.d. samples x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_n sampled from some unknown Bernoulli random variable XX. Let rr denote the “ground truth” probability of succcess for a given Bernoulli trial, so P(X=1)=rP(X = 1) = r and P(X=0)=1−rP(X = 0) = 1-r. The variable rr parameterizes our distribution, and our goal is to estimate rr from the samples. Intuitively, you might see that the sample mean θ̂n=1n∑i=1nxi\hat \theta_n = \frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n x_i is a good estimator for rr. We can make this more rigorous, and also expand our estimation techniques to include a prior distribution over the parameters of our model, using maximum likelihood (ML) and maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation. Machine learning tends to use a lot of maximum likelihood estimation – if you’ve ever used cross-entropy loss, you’ve done a form of ML estimation. MAP estimation is still good to know about and comes up a more frequently in statistical learning.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Setup: Let random variable 𝐗=[X1,X2,…,Xn]\mathbf X = [X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n]. Then for discrete XiX_i we would have joint PMF p𝐗(𝐱;θ)=p𝐗(x1,…,xn;θ)p_{\mathbf X} (\mathbf x; \theta) = p_{\mathbf X}(x_1, \dots, x_n; \theta) for discrete random variables or joint PDF f𝐗(𝐱;θ)f_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x; \theta).

The vector 𝐱\mathbf x represents a series of measurements. Then the maximum likelihood estimate of θ\theta is

θ̂n=argmaxθp𝐗(𝐱;θ) \hat \theta_n = \arg\max_{\theta} p_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x; \theta)

For i.i.d. X1,…,XnX_1, \dots, X_n, we can apply the independence property to get

θ̂n=argmaxθ∏i=1npX(xi;θ) \hat \theta_n = \arg\max_{\theta} \prod_{i=1}^n p_X(x_i; \theta)

Log transformation trick: The computationalists among you might be getting nervous because the product of many probabilities pX(xi;θ)∈[0,1]p_X(x_i; \theta) \in [0, 1] will become very small – too small to represent properly with floats! Fortunately you can just take the log\log of the product we want to maximize. Since log(z)\log(z) is a monotonic function of zz, maximizing log(z)\log(z) is equivalent to maximizing zz itself. Applying the logarithm,

θ̂n=argmaxθ∑i=1nlogpX(xi;θ) \hat \theta_n = \arg\max_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_X(x_i; \theta)

Instead of dealing with products over near-zero numbers, we now deal with the sum over (potentially very large) negative numbers arising from logpX(⋅)\log p_X(\cdot). The high-anxiety computationalists among you might still be worried, since we still face numerical instability when pX(⋅)=0p_X(\cdot) = 0 leading to log0=−∞\log 0 = -\infty. The standard fix for that is to add a small number ϵ\epsilon (often ϵ=10−9\epsilon = 10^-9) to pX(⋅)p_X(\cdot) before the logarithm. That way, as long as pX(⋅)∈[0,1]p_X(\cdot) \in [0, 1] and logpX(⋅)∈[−∞,0]\log p_X(\cdot) \in [-\infty, 0], we will have log(pX(⋅)+ϵ)∈[log(ϵ),log(1+ϵ)]≈[−6,0]\log(p_X(\cdot)+\epsilon) \in [\log(\epsilon), \log(1+\epsilon)] \approx [-6, 0].

Deriving ML Estimate for Bernoulli Random Variable Parameter: Recall our setup with a bunch of i.i.d. samples 𝐱=[x1,…,xn]\mathbf x = [x_1, \dots, x_n] from a Bernoulli random variable XX with unknown rate of success rr. Then we can apply the i.i.d. case of ML estimation, replacing generic θ\theta with rr:

r̂n=argmaxr∏i=1npX(xi;r) \hat r_n = \arg\max_{r} \prod_{i=1}^n p_X(x_i; r)

r̂n=argmaxr∏i=1n{r if xi=11−r if xi=0 \hat r_n = \arg\max_{r} \prod_{i=1}^n \begin{cases} r & \text{ if } x_i = 1 \\ 1-r & \text{ if } x_i = 0 \end{cases}

Let us denote the number of successful outcomes as mm. Then we get

r̂n=argmaxrrm(1−r)n−m⏟g(r;n,m) \hat r_n = \arg\max_r \underbrace{r^m (1-r)^{n-m}}_{g(r; n, m)}

To find the extreme points of g(r;n,m)g(r; n, m), let’s differentiate w.r.t. rr:

ddrg(r;n,m)=mrm−1(1−r)n−m−rm(n−m)(1−r)n−m−1 \frac{d}{dr} g(r; n, m) = m r^{m-1} (1-r)^{n-m} - r^m (n-m)(1-r)^{n-m-1}

Solving for zero, assuming non-zero rr:

mrm−1(1−r)n−m=rm(n−m)(1−r)n−m−1 m r^{m-1} (1-r)^{n-m} = r^m (n-m)(1-r)^{n-m-1}

m(1−r)=r(n−m) m (1-r) = r (n-m)

1−rr=n−mm \frac{1-r}{r} = \frac{n-m}{m}

1r=nm \frac{1}{r} = \frac{n}{m}

Therefore, r=m/nr = m/n, just as suggested by our intuition. Unlike our intuition, this approach allows us to calculate maximum likelihood estimators as long as we have some samples from the true distribution and a sense of the family of PMFs or PDFs to which the true distribution belongs.

Maximum a Posteriori Estimation

ML estimation is great in the limit of large nn, but for few samples, the model might do some silly things. Maximum a posteriori estimation incorporates a prior distribution over parameter θ\theta in the estimation process. Instead of maximizing the likelihood of the observed data given the parameter (as in ML estimation), maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation maximizes the probability of the parameters given the observed data. To do this, we apply Bayes theorem and our prior over θ\theta to flip from P(x;θ)P(x; \theta) to P(θ|x)P(\theta | x).

Bayes Theorem: P(B|A)=P(A|B)P(B)P(A)P(B | A) = P(A | B) \frac{P(B)}{P(A)}

Maximum a Posteriori Estimation: Let θ̂MAP\hat \theta_{MAP} represent the maximum a posteriori estimate of parameter θ\theta given samples {xi}i=1N\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N. Let p(θ)p(\theta) represent our prior distribution over θ\theta, and similar for p(x|θ)p(x | \theta) and p(x)p(x). Then

θ̂MAP=argmaxθf(θ|{x}i=1N) \hat \theta_{MAP} = \arg\max_{\theta} f(\theta | \{x\}_{i=1}^N)

⇔θ̂MAP=argmaxθf({xi}i=1N|θ)f(θ)f({xi}i=1N) \iff \hat \theta_{MAP} = \arg\max_{\theta} f(\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N | \theta) \frac{f(\theta)}{f(\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N)}

As before, the optimization objective works for PMFs p(⋅)p(\cdot) as well as PDFs f(⋅)f(\cdot).

1.3: Shannon N-Gram Models

Congratulations, you have acquired the basics of mathematical prediction! We have the tools to rigorously understand some of the earliest computational language models in history: Shannon’s N-grams! As citizens of the information age, we bask in the reflected glory of Shannon’s “greatest masters thesis of all time” on information theory. As he was formalizing his theories of communication over noisy channels and the like, Shannon was actively experimenting with ways to model the probability distribution of language.

Language is tricky, since the number of possible kk-long sequences of characters from a 26 character alphabet is, well, 26k26^k (a big number – so big that some dramatic scientists call it an “explosion”!). Since modern computers had yet to be developed, Shannon started by ignoring the fact that all previous characters x1,…,xk−1x_1, \dots, x_{k-1} in a sequence could concievably change the probability of the next character xkx_k. Instead, he restricted his models to N-grams – what’s the probability pθ(xN|x1,…,xN−1)p_{\theta}(x_N | x_1, \dots, x_{N-1})?

1-gram model

The 1-gram model is a great starting point because we are just interested in modelling the PMF pθ(x)p_{\theta}(x). The simplest way to parameterize this is as a categorical distribution – a generalization of the Bernoulli distributions we talked about earlier. θ\theta just needs to have 26 values representing the probability of each character in English. Just like with the Bernoulli distribution, if we have no prior and want to do ML estimation, we can just take the frequency of each character in our corpus as the probability of that character. If you really want you can re-derive it, but it’s basically the same as before.

Now we have a very simple model of language, and it’s good enough to do some interesting stuff with “typical sequences” and channel capacity. But if you sample sequences xi∼p(xi)x_i \sim p(x_i), you get gibberish because it cannot take any context into account:

1-gram approximation of english (symbols independent but with frequencies of English text): 

    OCRO HLI RGWR NMIELWIS EU LL NBNESEBYA TH EEI ALHENHTTPA OOBTTVA NAH BRL

2-gram model

The 2-gram model tries to estimate pθ(xN|xN−1)p_{\theta}(x_N | x_{N-1}). You can think of θ\theta as a big table or matrix.

[p(a|a)p(b|a)…p(z|a)p(a|b)p(b|b)…p(z|b)⋮⋮⋱⋮p(a|z)p(b|z)…p(z|z)] \begin{bmatrix} p(a | a) & p(b | a) & \dots & p(z | a) \\ p(a | b) & p(b | b) & \dots & p(z | b) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ p(a | z) & p(b | z) & \dots & p(z | z) \end{bmatrix}

Now we need to estimate 262=67626^2 = 676 frequencies from our dataset. We can just calculate the frequency of each of the 676 bigrams in English, which would give us the following matrix:

[p(a,a)p(b,a)…p(z,a)p(a,b)p(b,b)…p(z,b)⋮⋮⋱⋮p(a,z)p(b,z)…p(z,z)] \begin{bmatrix} p(a, a) & p(b, a) & \dots & p(z, a) \\ p(a, b) & p(b, b) & \dots & p(z, b) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ p(a, z) & p(b, z) & \dots & p(z, z) \end{bmatrix}

Then normalize each row to get back to the desired original conditional distribution matrix.

2-gram model of English: 

    ON IE ANTSOUTINYS ARE T INCTORE ST BE S DEAMY ACHIN D ILONASIVE TUCOOWE AT TEASONARE FUSO TIZIN ANDY TOBE SEACE CTISBE

Still not quite GPT-4, but it looks a bit more like normal English.

3-gram model

Hopefully you’re starting to see the pattern – for the 3-gram model, we need to estimate the probabilities of 263=1757626^3 = 17576 3-grams. Despite his lack of modern computing technology, Claude managed it:

3-gram model of English: 

    IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS GROCID PANDENOME OF DEMONSTURES OF THE REPTAGIN IS REGOACTIONA OF CRE

Not a bad language model for pre-WWII!

Beyond N-gram models

The combinatorial explosion of the number of parameters one must estimate for NN-gram models makes them prohibitively expensive to scale. Fortunately, there’s an entire discipline called “artificial intelligence” – especially the subfield of machine learning – that has made some progress on rendering problems with combinatorial explosions tractable!
